# Regulation, standards and governance practice for agent memory in finance

Facet tag: `regulation`. Source: regulation scout partial (`partials/regulation.md`, compiled 2026-09-24), lightly edited at synthesis on 2026-09-24. The main review is `review.md`: §2 maps these obligations onto memory operations × governance properties, and §8 sets out the cross-facet gaps. **Every URL was accessed 2026-09-24 unless marked otherwise.**
Status is given "as of 2026-09-24". Nothing here is legal advice. Items needing a lawyer are collected in §6.

**Synthesis edits (2026-09-24).**
1. **SR 26-2 / OCC Bulletin 2026-13 (2026-04-17).** The scout's [V] reading of the Federal Reserve and OCC pages has been *independently corroborated* by the orchestrator via multiple law-firm and industry sources.
2. **Reg. (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI).** The orchestrator independently corroborated the following via multiple law-firm and industry sources:
   - published in the OJ on **2026-07-24**;
   - in force **2026-07-27**;
   - Annex III high-risk obligations moved to **2027-12-02**.

   The scout's secondaries disagreed on the OJ date (24 July per iubenda, 29 July per Orrick). It is resolved here to **publication on 24 July 2026**, which is consistent with entry into force on 27 July. The label stays **[S]**, because the OJ text itself was not machine-readable from this environment. Final article wording remains on the §6 list.

5. **Model risk.** SR 11-7, named in the project brief, is superseded by SR 26-2 (§1d). Model-risk framing for US banks should refer to SR 26-2 and its GenAI/agentic carve-out.

## 2. Retention vs erasure vs information barriers: how the tensions look

The PI's hypothesis is that retention mandates, erasure/minimisation and information barriers pull against each other inside agent memory. What I found supports it with qualifications: the laws themselves contain partial reconciliation mechanisms. The unresolved part is **classification**, meaning which agent-memory items are "records", and **architecture**.

**T1. Retention vs erasure (EU-only case).** GDPR Art. 17(3)(b) exempts processing required by **Union or Member State law** [Vm]. MiFID II Art. 16(7), Del. Reg. 2017/565 Art. 72 and AMLR Art. 77 are Union law, so where agent memory contains in-scope records, a "forget me" request can lawfully be answered by *restricting and archiving* rather than deleting until the retention period ends. The AMLR goes further: it **mandates deletion at expiry** (Art. 77(3)) [Vm]. That is a statutory retain-then-forget schedule an agent-memory design can encode directly. GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) still limits the *operational* recall layer: data kept only to satisfy MiFID has no lawful purpose being recalled into unrelated future advice (my reading of purpose limitation, Art. 5(1)(b)).

**T2. Retention vs erasure (cross-border).** SEC 17a-4 and FINRA 4511 are **not** Union or Member State law. For EU data subjects served by a US-registered broker-dealer, 17(3)(b) does not obviously cover US retention. Firms would have to rely on 17(3)(e) (legal claims) or argue the Art. 6(1)(f)/Art. 17(1)(a) necessity analysis. **This is my legal reading, not a sourced conclusion; see §6.** California's CCPA exception is broader ("comply with a legal obligation", any law) [V]. The CEF 2025 report shows EU DPAs see controllers struggling exactly here: balancing against other legal obligations, retention-period determination, and backups [V].

**T3. Immutability vs rectification and forgetting.** 17a-4(f)(2) (audit trail or WORM) and Del. Reg. 2017/565 Art. 72(1) (prior contents "easily ascertained", no other manipulation) require that **modifications preserve history** [Vm/S]. GDPR Art. 16 and 5(1)(d) require inaccurate data to be rectified, and Colorado SB 26-189 adds a consumer right to correct inputs [V]. The two are compatible if "modify" is implemented as *supersede with a version history retained in a compliance archive* while the *recall layer* serves only the current version. This matches the long-standing blockchain-vs-GDPR discussion (Kuner et al. editorial, doi:10.1093/idpl/ipy009; the EDPB's final blockchain guidelines, July 2026, final wording not read). Crypto-shredding appears repeatedly as the bridge between immutability and erasure (per abstract, arXiv:2606.18497).

**T4. "Forget" that doesn't forget.** Soft-deleted vectors remain reconstructible (arXiv:2606.18497, per abstract: e.g. 99% facial-identity recovery). Deleted records leave measurable residual influence on RAG context (arXiv:2608.20352, per abstract). Parameter-level unlearning does not map onto legal erasure (arXiv:2412.06966; see also doi:10.1016/j.clsr.2023.105885 and arXiv:2307.03941). Combined with the EDPB's CEF finding that "anonymisation as a workaround" and backups are recurring failures [V], the implication is that an agent-memory **forget** operation needs *verifiable* deletion across index, cache, summaries/derived memories and backups. Output suppression (guardrails) should not be presented as erasure.

**T5. Logging obligations create new retained personal data.** AI Act Art. 12/19/26(6) (≥6 months; for FIs folded into financial-services documentation) [Vm], DORA RTS Art. 12 (tamper- and deletion-protected logs) [Vm], ESMA ¶24 (records of data sources and modifications over time) [V], FINRA's "track and log agent actions and decisions" [V], and the FSB's "enhanced documentation and logging… prompt versioning… chain-of-thought logging" [V] all push toward **logging recall events**. A recall log that stores retrieved memory content duplicates personal data outside the memory store, so erasing the memory does not erase the log. Art. 19(1)/26(6) explicitly defer to data-protection law on retention [Vm], but no source I found says how to log *which memory was recalled* without copying it. Hashes or pointers plus a retained archive are the obvious pattern (my observation). The accountability-privacy paradox is argued in a Zenodo preprint (LAVR; not in `papers.jsonl`, not peer reviewed; cited by name only).

**T6. Shared memory vs information barriers.** §15(g), FINRA 5280(b) and MAR Art. 9 turn on *policies and arrangements that effectively prevent information flow* [V]. A shared, relevance-ranked memory across desks or clients is structurally a flow channel. Purpose-bound memory mediation work reports that relevance-based retrieval leaked 53.0% of private items and contextual-integrity prompting still left 32.6% (arXiv:2506.22815, per abstract, programming-agent setting, not finance). The Brewer–Nash Chinese Wall policy (doi:10.1109/secpri.1989.36295) gives the formal model: access depends on access *history*. Under GDPR this is also a purpose-limitation issue (Art. 5(1)(b)): client A's data recalled for client B. **I found no regulator guidance and no academic paper that addresses information barriers for shared AI memory or RAG indices**, which is a clear gap.

**T7. Retaining context for fairness and explainability vs minimisation.** Suitability/KYC, Consumer Duty, UK Art. 22C review rights (from 2026-02-05), Colorado's 30-day adverse-outcome explanation and 3-year records, and ECOA adverse-action reasons all *require keeping* the memory state that drove a decision [V/M]. SR 26-2 now excludes agentic AI, so US banks lack a model-risk hook for this [V]. The replay study in arXiv:2608.11344 (per abstract) indicates decisions cannot be reliably re-derived later through hosted APIs, so the actual recalled context must be snapshotted. That strengthens the retention side.

**Resolution patterns found in sources:**
- Statutory exception: GDPR 17(3)(b) (EU law), CCPA 105(d).
- Statutory deletion at end of retention: AMLR 77(3).
- Folding AI logs into existing FS documentation: AI Act 19(2)/26(6).
- Pseudonymisation with relative identifiability: CJEU SRB; EDPB draft 02/2026; omnibus proposal.
- Crypto-shredding: arXiv:2606.18497.
- Append-only versioning: 17a-4(f)(2) audit-trail option.
- Segregation / history-dependent access control: Brewer–Nash; MaaS purpose-bound mediation.

**Not found:** any official text that classifies agent memory as "books and records" or states when a memory entry is a "communication".

## 5. Gaps I noticed (my observations, confidence in brackets)

1. **No regulator classifies agent memory.** Nothing I read says whether an agent's persistent memory entries are "books and records" or "communications" (17a-4(b)(4), MiFID 16(7)), or "logs" (AI Act Art. 12). FINRA names "unintentionally store" as a risk but stops there. This classification decides which side of every tension applies. [high]
2. **Information barriers for shared AI memory/RAG are unaddressed** in regulation, supervisory guidance and scholarship. Brewer–Nash exists (1989); I found no agent-memory implementation or evaluation of history-dependent recall control, and no discussion of whether an AI component belongs on a MAR Art. 18 insider list. [high for absence in what I searched; medium overall, since law-review coverage via these tools is weak]
3. **Recall logging vs minimisation has no stated method.** The AI Act, DORA, FSB and FINRA all push for logging, but none says how to log *what was recalled* without replicating personal data. [medium-high]
4. **Verifiable erasure for agent memory.** Evidence of failure is emerging (vector-DB tombstones, residual RAG influence), but there is no accepted test for "the agent has forgotten X" that a DPA or auditor would accept. This overlaps the evaluation facet. [high]
5. **US model-risk vacuum.** SR 26-2 explicitly scopes out GenAI and agentic AI, and the promised AI RFI had not appeared by 2026-09-24. PRA SS1/23 appears not to carve AI out (to verify). This creates a US/UK divergence that affects how agent-memory changes are validated. [high for the fact; medium for its significance]
6. **Cross-border retention conflict** (US 17a-4 vs EU GDPR 17(3)(b) "Union or Member State law") is under-analysed in what I found. [medium]
7. **Timeline mismatch.** EU high-risk obligations moved to 2027-12-02, while Colorado's replacement statute takes effect 2027-01-01, CPPA ADMT compliance 2027-01-01 and AMLR 2027-07-10. Firms face a 2027 cluster; the EU AI Act is no longer the first mover. [high]
8. **Regulation-as-code work targets rule text, not memory operations.** No paper encodes retention classes, barrier labels and purpose tags as machine-checkable policies on memory writes and reads. [medium]
